# Stable Matching

Gale–Shapley, worked both directions

---

## Three ideas, before anything else

- stable matching
- deferred acceptance
- proposer-optimal

Get these three straight now — the worked example runs on all three.

---

## Stable matching

Six people pair off. It doesn't have to make anyone thrilled — it just has
to survive contact with temptation: no one currently paired should be able
to find someone else who'd *also* rather be with them than their own
current match.

That no-mutual-regret property is **stability**, and a matching that has
it is a **stable matching**.

---

## Deferred acceptance

When someone asks you out and you're not sure, "yes, for now" is a
reasonable answer — you're free to trade up later if someone better asks,
and whoever you're currently seeing finds out by being the one who gets
let go.

That's exactly how this week's algorithm works, and it has a name:
**deferred acceptance** — every "yes" is provisional until nobody better
shows up.

---

## Proposer-optimal

Here's the twist: whichever side does the asking, as a group, ends up with
the best partner they could possibly get in *any* stable arrangement.
Asking isn't just brave — it's structurally an advantage.

Economists call that result **proposer-optimality**, and it's the sharpest
claim this week's theorem makes.

---

## Now, back to the point

A small matchmaking pool: three Askers, three Matches. Everyone ranks
everyone on the other side. We'll run it once with the Askers proposing,
then run the *exact same preferences* with the Matches proposing instead.

---

## The preferences

| Asker | 1st choice | 2nd choice | 3rd choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robin | Jordan | Casey | Morgan |
| Sam | Casey | Jordan | Morgan |
| Drew | Jordan | Casey | Morgan |

| Match | 1st choice | 2nd choice | 3rd choice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jordan | Sam | Robin | Drew |
| Casey | Robin | Sam | Drew |
| Morgan | Robin | Sam | Drew |

---

## Run 1: Askers propose

| Round | Proposals | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Robin→Jordan, Sam→Casey, Drew→Jordan | Jordan prefers Robin to Drew — holds Robin, rejects Drew. Casey holds Sam. |
| 2 | Drew→Casey (his next choice) | Casey prefers Sam to Drew — keeps Sam, rejects Drew. |
| 3 | Drew→Morgan (his last choice) | Morgan's only offer — holds Drew. |

**Result: Robin–Jordan, Sam–Casey, Drew–Morgan.** Robin and Sam both got
their first choice.

---

## Run 2: Matches propose instead

Same six people, same preferences — just flip who does the asking.

| Round | Proposals | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Jordan→Sam, Casey→Robin, Morgan→Robin | Robin prefers Casey to Morgan — holds Casey, rejects Morgan. Sam holds Jordan. |
| 2 | Morgan→Sam (her next choice) | Sam prefers Jordan to Morgan — keeps Jordan, rejects Morgan. |
| 3 | Morgan→Drew (her last choice) | Drew's only offer — holds Morgan. |

**Result: Robin–Casey, Sam–Jordan, Drew–Morgan.**

---

## Same people, same preferences, different outcome

| Person | Askers propose | Matches propose |
|---|---|---|
| Robin | Jordan (1st choice) | Casey (2nd choice) |
| Sam | Casey (1st choice) | Jordan (2nd choice) |
| Jordan | Robin (2nd choice) | Sam (1st choice) |
| Casey | Sam (2nd choice) | Robin (1st choice) |
| Drew / Morgan | matched to each other, either way | matched to each other, either way |

Both matchings are genuinely stable. Whoever proposes does better, as a
group, than whoever receives — every single time.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> Stable does not mean happy. It means nobody has an incentive to break
> the arrangement, given what everyone else is doing — and it means the
> side that asked got the better half of that arrangement.

---

## What the theorem doesn't say

- nothing about proposer-optimal vs. receiver-optimal outcomes being equal
- nothing about the surplus once matched — that's week 9
- nothing about what happens when preferences change after matching

## Tutorial 8

Run the algorithm by hand on a fresh preference table. Compare what you get
proposing from side A against proposing from side B — you've just seen why
they won't match.

## What today doesn't settle

The theorem proves the proposing side does better, but says nothing about
how a real market decides who plays that role. Week 7 couldn't explain who
breaks a standoff; this week, whoever a market simply *labels* the
proposer wins the same way, unearned.
