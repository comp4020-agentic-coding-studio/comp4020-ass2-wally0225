# Household Bargaining

Nash bargaining, and what actually gets negotiated once the matching is done

---

## Three ideas, before anything else

- cooperative surplus
- outside option
- Nash bargaining solution

Get these three straight now — the worked example runs on all three.

---

## Cooperative surplus

Two people living together can produce something neither could alone: rent
split two ways instead of one, errands that only need one trip, someone
home for the delivery. That extra value, on top of whatever either of you
would get living apart, is real, and it's up for grabs.

Economists call it **cooperative surplus** — value created by the pair that
neither side owns until it's split.

---

## Outside option

Before you can split anything, you both need to know what happens if the
split falls through — the apartment you'd get on your own, the roommate
you'd fall back to, the deal you'd settle for if this one collapsed. That's
your leverage, whether you ever say it out loud or not.

That fallback is your **outside option** — what you'd walk away with,
sometimes called your threat point.

---

## Nash bargaining solution

Given a surplus and two outside options, there's a specific way to split
it that gives each of you the largest possible gain over walking away,
while satisfying a short list of properties nobody would object to: it
doesn't waste any surplus, it treats identical positions identically, and
neither side can improve their share just by dressing up the negotiation.

That split has a name: the **Nash bargaining solution** — one answer, not
a range, once the surplus and the outside options are fixed.

---

## Now, back to the point

Alex and Sam are moving in together.

---

## The numbers

Living together instead of separately is worth $100 a month, combined —
that's the cooperative surplus. But each of them has a fallback if this
doesn't work out.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Combined surplus from living together | $100/month |
| Alex's outside option (a cheaper solo lease) | $20/month |
| Sam's outside option (moving in with a sibling) | $10/month |

---

## Splitting it

The Nash bargaining solution gives each side their outside option, then
splits whatever's left over *equally*, since neither side's claim on the
remainder is stronger than the other's.

| Step | Alex | Sam |
|---|---|---|
| Outside option, guaranteed first | $20 | $10 |
| Surplus left after both outside options ($100 − $20 − $10 = $70) | | |
| Split of the $70 remainder | +$35 | +$35 |
| **Total share** | **$55** | **$45** |

Alex ends up with more — not because Alex is more persuasive, but because
Alex's fallback was worth more to begin with.

---

## What happens when an outside option moves

Six months later, Alex gets offered a much better solo lease: the outside
option jumps from $20 to $40. Nothing about how much either of them likes
living together has changed.

| Step | Alex | Sam |
|---|---|---|
| New outside option | $40 | $10 |
| Surplus left ($100 − $40 − $10 = $50) | | |
| Split of the $50 remainder | +$25 | +$25 |
| **New total share** | **$65** | **$35** |

Alex's share rose by $10 — exactly matching the rise in Alex's outside
option. Sam didn't do anything wrong, and nothing was miscommunicated.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> Nash bargaining doesn't ask who's right. It asks what each of you could
> get by leaving — and splits the rest down the middle.

---

## Why "just communicate better" misses it

A couple that renegotiates chores every few months and always ends up
lopsided isn't failing at communication. If one side's outside option
quietly strengthened — a promotion, a rent-controlled unit that opened up,
a friend with a spare room — the model says the split *should* move in
their favor, communication or no communication.

## What today doesn't settle

The model takes both outside options as known, true numbers and hands them
straight to the formula. It has no term for what happens when an outside
option is a claim rather than a fact — and "I could easily find another
place" is exactly the kind of statement week 3 said you can't take at face
value just because someone says it with confidence.
