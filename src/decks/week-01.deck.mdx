# Love, Markets, and Other Failures

A semester spent taking one metaphor seriously

---

## A market is

- a scarce thing
- allocated among people who want it
- under rules nobody fully controls

Housing is a market. University admission is a market.

## What it costs us

The claim is **not** that romance is "just" economics.

It's narrower: wherever information is uneven and outcomes depend on what
others do, markets fail in specific, nameable ways.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> The semester is a tour of those failures, in order of how badly they
> hurt.

## A market failing, on schedule

Before 1950, US hospitals hired new doctors through interviews, then
offers. No prices adjusted — just **timing**.

| Year | What hospitals did |
|------|---------------------|
| pre-1945 | Interview, then wait for an offer |
| 1945 | Offers race earlier every year |
| 1949 | "Exploding" offers — decide in a day, no comparison possible |
| early 1950s | A centralized clearinghouse fixes the timing |

Nobody was irrational. Everyone was reacting correctly to everyone else.

---

## Same shape, different market

| Residency market | Courtship market |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| Interview → wait → compare offers | Meet → date a while → decide |
| A competitor jumps the gun | One side asks to "lock it down" early |
| Exploding offer: decide before comparing | Ultimatum: decide before dating anyone else |
| Clearinghouse restores comparison | Week 8: what a fair "compare all at once" looks like |

---

## One thing this model won't explain

The clearinghouse fixed the residency market's **timing**. It never asked
whether earlier certainty was worth anything on its own.

This course's model does the same: it prices the inefficiency of being
rushed, but has no term for the anxiety, or the relief, itself.

**Week 12 comes back to this.**
