# Repeated Games & Trust

Why the same game plays out differently once you expect to play it again tomorrow

---

## Four ideas, before anything else

- one-shot game
- the folk theorem
- shadow of the future
- tit-for-tat

Get these four straight now — the tournament next uses all of them.

---

## One-shot game

If you were only ever going to see this person once, there'd be no reason
to be generous, forgiving, or even honest about small things — nothing you
do today can be answered tomorrow, because there is no tomorrow.

A game played exactly once, with no future round to reward or punish what
happened, is a **one-shot game** — and in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma,
selfishness is the only equilibrium.

---

## Shadow of the future

The moment you expect to see the same person again — same friend group,
same apartment, same relationship — every choice today casts a shadow
forward. Being unkind now can be answered later; being generous now can be
banked on later, too.

Economists call how much weight today's choice gives to future rounds the
**shadow of the future** — and it's the single variable that decides
whether cooperation can survive.

---

## The folk theorem

Here's the striking part: once the shadow of the future is long enough,
it's not just cooperation that becomes possible — almost *any* pattern of
behavior both sides can live with becomes a sustainable equilibrium,
provided each side can credibly punish the other for breaking it.

That result is the **folk theorem** — repetition doesn't just allow
cooperation, it explodes the number of equilibria the game can settle
into.

---

## Tit-for-tat

Faced with that explosion of possible equilibria, one simple rule keeps
winning in practice: start by cooperating, then do back to your partner
exactly what they just did to you. Nothing more elaborate than that.

That rule is **tit-for-tat**, and its success isn't about being clever —
it's about being instantly readable, quick to forgive, and impossible to
exploit for long.

## Now, back to the point

Two friends who split rent for years, tested by one bad month.

---

## One bad month, two possible worlds

| Scenario | Neither has a future together | Both expect years more together |
|---|---|---|
| One skips their share | nothing stops them — it's the last round | risks the other retaliating for months, or years |
| Both keep paying honestly | pure luck, or pure decency | a *stable equilibrium* — quitting now costs more than it gains |
| What sustains cooperation | nothing in the model | the shadow of the future alone |

Nothing about either person changed between the columns. Only how long
they expect to keep playing did.

---

## Running tit-for-tat on the bad month

| Month | Friend A pays share? | Friend B pays share? | What tit-for-tat does next |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–5 | yes | yes | keep cooperating |
| 6 | no (lost their job) | yes | B notices — will mirror the defection next month |
| 7 | yes (job back, apologizes) | no (mirroring month 6) | A notices B's retaliation — mirrors back |
| 8 | no (mirroring month 7) | yes (forgives — A's month 7 was a one-off) | back to cooperating |

One missed month costs exactly one round of retaliation, then the
relationship self-corrects — *if* both sides are reading tit-for-tat and
not something harsher.

---

## What if a mistake gets misread?

Swap Friend B's rule from tit-for-tat to grim trigger: defect forever after
the first defection, no matter what happens next.

| Month | Friend A | Friend B (grim trigger) | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | no (lost their job) | still yes | — |
| 7 | yes (job back) | no, and no round ever again | cooperation is over, permanently |

Same bad month. A stricter rule turns one missed payment into the end of
the arrangement — the strategy chosen, not the mistake itself, decided
how much a single bad month was allowed to cost.

---

> Trust isn't a feeling you either have or don't. It's what a repeated game
> looks like from the inside, when the shadow of the future is long enough
> to make cooperation pay for itself.

## Back to week 9

A negotiated split (week 9) isn't self-enforcing on its own — nothing
stops either side from quietly grabbing more the moment they think it's
the last round. What actually holds a split together, month after month,
is exactly this week's shadow of the future: the expectation of enough
future rounds that breaking the deal now costs more than it gains.

## What today doesn't settle

The folk theorem proves cooperation *can* be an equilibrium once the
shadow of the future is long enough — but so can almost anything else two
sides can credibly threaten each other into. The model has no way to say
which equilibrium two real people actually land in, and no built-in
account of how much forgiveness a strategy should carry before one bad
month becomes a permanent grudge.

That's week 7's gap again, one level up: week 7 couldn't say who breaks a
standoff between two people on one night; this week, the same missing
ingredient decides which of many possible relationships two people spend
years living inside.
