# Mechanism Design for Two

Working backwards from the outcome you want to the rules that would produce it

---

## Three ideas, before anything else

- mechanism design
- incentive compatibility
- individual rationality

Get these three straight now — Assignment 2 is built entirely out of them.

---

## Mechanism design

Every week so far handed you the rules and asked what happens. This week
flips it: you already know what you want — chores split fairly, plans made
without a fight — and the question is what rule would actually produce
that, on purpose, instead of by luck.

That reverse direction — outcome first, rules second — is **mechanism
design**.

---

## Incentive compatibility

A rule that only works if everyone follows it honestly isn't really a
rule, it's a hope. "We'll each be truthful about how tired we are" sounds
fair right up until being truthful stops being anyone's best move.

A rule that a self-interested person would still want to follow, purely
out of self-interest, is **incentive-compatible** — it doesn't need anyone
to be good, only rational.

---

## Individual rationality

There's a second bar a rule has to clear, separate from incentive
compatibility: it has to be better than just opting out. A perfectly
game-proof rule that one side would rather ignore entirely isn't a
mechanism, it's a rule nobody's actually playing.

That second bar — the rule has to beat the outside option of not playing
at all — is **individual rationality**.

---

## Now, back to the point

A couple keeps fighting about who initiates date-night planning. Someone
proposes a rule.

---

## Rule one: "whoever's less busy plans it"

| Property | Does it hold? |
|---|---|
| Incentive-compatible? | No — claiming to be busy is free and unverifiable, so both sides have a reason to claim it |
| Individually rational? | Yes, if either side actually believes it'll be followed |
| What happens in practice | Both sides start reporting "so busy this week," and nobody plans anything |

The rule sounds fair. It's gamed the moment it's said out loud, because
"busy" is cheap talk — exactly the problem week 3 named, showing up again
in a rule instead of a profile.

---

## Rule two: "we alternate, strictly, no exceptions"

| Property | Does it hold? |
|---|---|
| Incentive-compatible? | Yes — there's nothing to lie about; whose turn it is doesn't depend on anyone's claim |
| Individually rational? | Only if the burden of planning is roughly similar in a genuinely busy week and a quiet one |
| What happens in practice | Works most weeks; strains exactly when one person is *actually* overwhelmed and the rule can't tell that apart from someone gaming rule one |

---

## Rule three: alternate, with a once-a-month swap either side can call

| Property | Does it hold? |
|---|---|
| Incentive-compatible? | Yes — calling a swap costs a scarce token, so it isn't worth spending unless the week is genuinely bad |
| Individually rational? | Yes — strictly better than either rule one or straight alternation for both sides |
| What happens in practice | Genuine bad weeks get covered; the token's scarcity is what stops it from becoming rule one again |

The fix wasn't asking people to be more honest. It was making honesty the
cheapest option, by attaching a real cost to claiming an exception.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> A mechanism doesn't ask anyone to be good. It arranges things so that
> being good and being self-interested point the same direction.

## Tutorial 11

Swap your Assignment 2 draft rule with a partner and try, deliberately, to
find a way a rational, self-interested participant could follow it to the
letter while defeating its intent. Revise in response to what they find.

---

## What today doesn't settle

Every rule above assumed someone gets to propose it and the other side
just responds. But in a real relationship, there's no neutral outside
designer choosing the best rule for both of you — whoever proposes the
"ground rules" is also a player who has to live under them, and has every
reason to propose a rule that's incentive-compatible for the *other*
person and quietly favorable to themselves.

That's the same missing ingredient this course keeps finding under a new
name: week 7 couldn't say who breaks a standoff, week 8 couldn't say who
gets labeled the proposer, week 9 couldn't say whose outside-option claim
gets believed — and this week, mechanism design can't say who gets to
design the mechanism in the first place.

**Week 12 is where this, and every prior week's gap, come back at once.**
