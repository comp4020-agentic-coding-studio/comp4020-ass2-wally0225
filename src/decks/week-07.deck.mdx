# The Cost of Asking

Why someone has to move first, and why that's expensive

---

## Four ideas, before anything else

- revealed interest
- war of attrition
- coordination game
- inefficient equilibrium

Get these four straight now — everything today, and the midterm afterward,
reuses them.

---

## Revealed interest

The moment you text first, you've told the other person something you
can't take back: you care enough to move. If they don't feel the same,
you've just handed them information they can use to feel superior, stall,
or walk away — for free.

Economists call that cost of moving first **revealed interest** — acting
reveals what silence hides.

---

## That's a costly signal, pointed at a person

Week 3 called a claim credible when it would be expensive to fake.
Moving first *is* that, except the claim isn't about you — it's "I want
this, with you, specifically" — and the expense isn't money, it's the risk
of being turned down in a way that's now on record.

Nothing new is being invented here. It's the same mechanism, aimed
outward instead of inward.

---

## War of attrition

If both sides know that moving first is costly and staying silent is free,
both sides have an incentive to out-wait each other — even though someone
eventually has to move for anything to happen at all.

That standoff, where both sides burn time hoping the other blinks first, is
a **war of attrition**.

---

## Coordination game

Whoever moves first isn't really the point — what matters is that *someone*
does, and both sides would rather that be settled quickly than dragged out.
Getting two people to a "someone goes" outcome, any outcome, is the real
problem.

That's a **coordination game**: multiple workable outcomes, and no
built-in way to pick one.

---

## Inefficient equilibrium

Here's the uncomfortable part: "both wait" isn't a bug in the model. Given
that the other side is staying silent, neither side gains anything by
being the one who breaks it — that's a genuine equilibrium, nobody wants
to unilaterally deviate.

It's simply a **worse** equilibrium than "someone moves," for both people,
at the same time it's perfectly stable. Economists call that an
**inefficient equilibrium**: individually rational, collectively costly.

## Now, back to the point

Two people, three days after a good first date. Neither has texted.

---

## The standoff

| Day | Person A | Person B | What each is thinking |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | silent | silent | "if I text now I look too eager" |
| 2 | silent | silent | "if they liked it, they'd text" |
| 3 | silent | silent | "maybe neither of us actually liked it" |

Both would rather someone text than nobody. Neither wants to be the one who
does.

---

## What "both wait" actually costs

| Combination | A's cost | B's cost | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Both wait | nothing | nothing | stable — and the worst outcome for both |
| A texts, B replies well | reveals interest, risked rejection | nothing | best outcome, but A bore the whole risk |
| A texts, B doesn't reply | reveals interest, gets rejected | nothing | A pays the full cost alone |
| Both text at once | reveals interest, risked rejection | reveals interest, risked rejection | best outcome, and the risk is shared |

"Both wait" costs nothing and produces nothing. It's still an equilibrium —
that's exactly the problem.

---

## Who actually moves first

The war of attrition's exit-timing result: when two sides value the
outcome differently, the side with more to lose from further delay moves
first — not the more confident one, the more invested one.

"Playing it cool" is, in this light, a deliberate attempt to hide how much
you'd lose by waiting, so you don't end up being the one who moves.

---

> The model can tell you a standoff is possible. It cannot tell you which
> of you is going to break it.

## What today doesn't settle

The war of attrition has more than one equilibrium — both wait, or one
moves — and nothing inside the model picks between them. Whatever actually
breaks a real standoff (etiquette, a script, plain impatience) comes from
outside the game entirely.

**Week 12 is where this, and every prior week's gap, come back at once.**
