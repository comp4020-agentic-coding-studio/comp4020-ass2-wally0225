# Exit Markets & the Redesign

Divorce as an exit market, and the collected bill for every gap this semester left open

---

## Three ideas, before anything else

- exit market
- sunk cost
- bargaining power

Get these three straight now — the capstone asks you to use all three on a
market of your own choosing.

---

## Exit market

Every market this semester assumed getting in was the hard part. But
knowing you can leave — and what that actually costs — is just as much a
market as finding someone in the first place: leaving means re-entering
week 2's search costs from scratch, at whatever age and circumstance
you're leaving from, against a pool that's moved on without you.

A market for finding a replacement for what you're about to give up,
complete with its own search costs and its own uncertain outside options,
is an **exit market**.

---

## Sunk cost

Four years of shared friends, a lease signed together, a routine that took
a year to build — none of it makes staying the right call going forward. A
purely forward-looking decision should only weigh what happens next, not
what's already been spent getting here. And yet what's already spent is
exactly what makes leaving feel unthinkable.

Money, time, or effort already spent — that a forward-looking decision
should ignore, but that keeps pulling people to stay anyway — is a **sunk
cost**.

---

## Bargaining power

Two people with identical facts — same outside options, same sunk costs,
same years invested — can land on wildly different outcomes, because one
of them can credibly threaten to walk and be believed, and the other
can't. Every model since week 9 has quietly assumed outside-option claims
are just true, handed straight to a formula.

The capacity to make your threat to leave, or your claim about what you're
owed, actually count for something is **bargaining power** — and naming it
is this week's whole point.

## Now, back to the point

Alex and Sam, four years past the split in week 9, aren't negotiating a
lease anymore. They're negotiating whether to stay together at all.

---

## What the exit decision has to model

| Cost | What it captures |
|---|---|
| Search cost | Re-entering the dating market from scratch, at a different age and life stage than the last time either of them searched |
| Sunk cost | Four years of shared friends, routines, and a signed lease — none of it relevant to what happens next, all of it hard to walk away from |
| Outside option | The value of life alone, or with someone else — exactly as self-reported and unverifiable as Alex's and Sam's outside-option claims back in week 9 |

---

## Same facts, different outcome

| Question | Alex has a real fallback (family nearby, no financial need for Sam) | Sam claims the identical fallback, falsely, and isn't caught |
|---|---|---|
| Who makes concessions to stay together? | Sam | Sam, again |
| What actually decided that | A genuine difference in outside options | Nothing but whether the claim was believed |

Nash bargaining, applied honestly here, can't tell these two rows apart.
Only bargaining power can — and the model has no variable for it.

Nine different "the model can't say X" moments. Nine sightings of the same
missing variable, wearing a different costume each week.

---

## What this framework never modeled

Naming bargaining power doesn't explain it. This model still has nothing
to say about *where* it comes from — why one partner's threat to leave is
credible and the other's isn't, why one person's claimed outside option
gets believed and another's doesn't.

That's not a gap next week fixes. There is no next week. It's the question
the capstone hands to you, for a market you choose yourself.
