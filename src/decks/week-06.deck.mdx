# The Secretary Problem

The one week this semester where the answer is exact

---

## Three ideas, before anything else

- optimal stopping
- the 1/e rule
- no recall

Get these three straight now — the scenario in a minute uses all of them.

---

## Optimal stopping

You're not choosing the best person you'll ever meet. You're choosing when
to stop meeting new people and commit to one, knowing you can't go back to
someone you already passed on.

Economists call that decision **optimal stopping**: the problem isn't who
to pick, it's when to stop looking.

---

## No recall

Once you've moved on from someone — stopped replying, let the moment pass —
you don't get to circle back and choose them later. They've moved on too.

That constraint is **no recall**, and it's what makes the problem hard: you
can't sample now and decide later.

---

## The 1/e rule

Given no recall and no idea how good later options will be, there's an
exact answer: spend the first ~37% of your expected dating life purely
gathering information — reject everyone, on principle — then commit to the
next person who beats everyone you've seen so far.

That threshold is the **1/e rule** (37% ≈ 1/e). It's not a guess. It's the
provably best strategy against an unknown distribution.

---

{/* _class: banner */}

## Now, back to the point

You expect to date roughly 20 people before settling down. Person #8 is the
best one yet. Do you commit?

---

## Running the rule

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| Expected pool | 20 people |
| Information-only phase | first ~7-8 (37% of 20) — reject everyone, just calibrate |
| Commit phase | from #8 onward, accept the first person who beats everyone seen so far |
| Person #8 | is inside the commit phase, and the best so far — the rule says yes |

---

## Why "you'll know when you know" fails

Gut instinct has no phase boundary — it says commit the moment someone
feels right, which might be person #2, before you've seen enough of the
distribution to know what "right" should even mean relative to.

The 1/e rule beats intuition specifically by making the calibration phase
mandatory, not optional.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

> The rule doesn't tell you who to pick. It tells you how much looking is
> enough before an opinion means anything.

Run the simulator against your own gut strategy first, then against the

## What today doesn't settle

The rule optimizes your stopping decision alone. It has no term for whether
the person you stop on would also stop on you — every guarantee here
quietly assumes the choice only runs one way.

**Week 8 starts answering that. Week 12 is where this, and Weeks 1, 3, 4,
and 5's gaps, all come back at once.**
