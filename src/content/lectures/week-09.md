The dishes were not the problem. A stable match creates a cooperative
surplus — everything the pair can produce together that neither could
alone — and the question week 8 left open is how that surplus gets split.
Nash's bargaining solution answers it under a small set of axioms
(efficiency, symmetry, invariance, independence of irrelevant alternatives)
and gives a unique split as a function of each side's outside option: what
they'd get by walking away.

This is where "market failure" stops being a metaphor for something that
happened before the match and becomes a description of what can go wrong
after it: a bad split isn't a communication problem, it's what happens when
one side's outside option quietly weakens and the other's doesn't.

The mechanics are almost embarrassingly simple once you see them: give each
side their outside option first, then split whatever surplus is left over
equally, since neither side has a stronger claim on the remainder than the
other. What makes household bargaining hard in practice isn't the formula —
it's that outside options aren't posted anywhere. They're renegotiated in
the dark, updated the moment one side's job, health, or family situation
shifts, and neither side can fully verify what the other would actually get
by walking away. A promotion, a rent-controlled unit that opens up, a
friend with a spare room: none of these show up as an announcement, but
each one moves the split, whether or not anyone says a word about it.

That last point is where this week quietly borrows from an earlier one. An
outside option is a claim — "I could easily find another place" — and week
3 already established that a claim is cheap unless something backs it up.
Nash bargaining assumes the outside options fed into the formula are true
numbers, not opening offers in a negotiation about the negotiation itself.
Week 10 picks up the version of this that plays out over years rather than
one sitting: what changes when the same two people split the same kind of
surplus over and over, and each side's behavior in round one is watched
before round two ever starts.

## Outline

- cooperative surplus, and where it comes from once a match already exists
- the Nash bargaining solution: the axioms, and the single split they pin
  down
- outside options as the actual variable, and why they move after matching
  without either side saying anything
- why "just communicate better" mistakes a bargaining-power problem for an
  information problem
- what happens when an outside option is a claim rather than a verified
  fact, and why the model can't tell the difference

## One thing this framework won't explain

Nash bargaining takes each side's outside option as a known, true number
and hands it straight to a formula. Real negotiations don't work that way:
an outside option is something one side *tells* the other, not something
either can fully verify in the moment. "I could easily find another place"
is a claim identical in shape to the cheap talk week 3 warned about — the
only difference is that here the stakes are the entire split of the
surplus, not whether someone replies to a first message.

Nothing about this rules out honesty. Most people probably do state
something close to their real outside option, for a reason that should
sound familiar by now: the relationship survives the negotiation, and
getting caught inflating a threat point is expensive in a way that a bad
opening line never was. But the model has no term for that cost, and no
way to tell a real outside option from a well-told story about one. Week
12 is where this, and every prior week's gap, come back at once.

## Further reading

- Nash, J. F. (1950).
  ["The Bargaining Problem."](https://www.haverford.edu/sites/default/files/Nash1950.pdf)
  _Econometrica_, 18(2), 155–162. The paper this week's solution and its
  axioms are drawn from directly — four properties (efficiency, symmetry,
  invariance, and independence of irrelevant alternatives) that together
  pin down exactly one split, not a range of plausible ones.
- Lundberg, S., & Pollak, R. A. (1996).
  ["Bargaining and Distribution in Marriage."](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.10.4.139)
  _Journal of Economic Perspectives_, 10(4), 139–158. The real-world
  application this week's household scenario is built on — a survey of
  what happens when economists stopped modeling a couple as one shared
  preference and started asking whose outside option is doing the work.
