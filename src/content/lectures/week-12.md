Every market needs an exit option or its participants aren't really
choosing to stay — they're trapped. This week treats separation the same
way it treated formation: search costs for re-entering the market from
scratch, sunk costs that keep people past the point a fresh analysis would
recommend, and the asymmetric information problem of never quite knowing,
from inside a relationship, what your own outside option is actually
worth.

Alex and Sam, the couple who negotiated a lease split back in week 9, are
now negotiating something bigger: whether to stay together at all. The
same three costs apply, and the same unverifiable-claim problem applies
too — except this time, the stakes of being believed are the whole
relationship, not just who pays more rent.

Then the semester turns the model on itself, on purpose. Every week since
week 7 has circled back to one unresolved question — "the model can't say
who decides" — under a different name: who breaks a standoff, who gets
labelled the proposer, whose outside-option claim gets believed, which
equilibrium two people settle into, who designs the rule. Collected side
by side, those aren't five separate gaps, and they're not even the first
five: weeks 1, 3, 4, 5, and 6 were quietly asking the same question the
whole time. They're one variable — bargaining power — that this semester's
toolkit never had a term for, showing up ten times under ten different
names.

That's the shape of the self-doubt this week asks for, and it's also the
shape of the capstone: not "the model is wrong," which is too easy to say
and explains nothing, but a specific place where a market framework goes
silent, for a case you choose yourself. The capstone is where you answer
that for us, not the other way around.

## Outline

- exit as a market with its own search costs and sunk costs, applied to
  Alex and Sam's decision to stay or leave
- why identical facts can produce different outcomes once bargaining power
  is added to Nash bargaining
- collecting ten weeks of "the model can't say who decides" into one
  missing variable
- what naming bargaining power still doesn't explain — where it comes from
- the capstone brief, and what "stands on its own" means for a project
  built to question its own premise

## One thing this framework won't explain

Giving the missing variable a name — bargaining power — doesn't explain
it. The model still has nothing to say about *where* bargaining power
itself comes from: why one partner's threat to leave is credible and the
other's isn't, why one person's outside-option claim gets believed and
another's doesn't. Naming the gap was always easier than closing it.

Laid end to end, this course's ten "the model can't say who decides"
moments were never ten separate failures. Week 7 couldn't say who breaks
a standoff. Week 8 couldn't say who gets labelled the proposer. Week 9
couldn't say whose outside-option claim gets believed. Week 10 couldn't
say which of many equilibria two people settle into. Week 11 couldn't say
who gets to design the mechanism. Even the earlier weeks fit the same
pattern one level down: week 1's value of not being rushed, week 3's
costliness that never guaranteed relevance, week 4's adverse selection
that couldn't distinguish its own causes, week 5's cost the honest type
still had to pay, week 6's secretary problem that only ever let one side
choose — every one of them is bargaining power, or its absence, wearing a
different costume. There is no week 13 for this gap to defer to. The
capstone is where you go find it in a market of your own choosing, and say
out loud where the model you build goes silent, instead of letting a
professor say it for you.

## Further reading

- Becker, G. S. (1973).
  ["A Theory of Marriage: Part I."](https://www.amherst.edu/system/files/media/1808/becker%20marriage.pdf)
  _Journal of Political Economy_, 81(4), 813–846. The paper this entire
  semester has been extending: the first attempt to model marriage
  formation and dissolution with the tools of price theory, marriage
  markets and all. Worth re-reading now that you can see what it does and
  doesn't say about who holds power inside the household it models.
- Stevenson, B., & Wolfers, J. (2006).
  ["Bargaining in the Shadow of the Law: Divorce Laws and Family
  Distress."](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10175/w10175.pdf)
  _Quarterly Journal of Economics_, 121(1), 267–288. Shows that a change in
  exit rights — unilateral divorce law — shifted real bargaining power
  inside existing marriages, measurably reducing domestic violence and
  female suicide, without anyone renegotiating a single explicit term.
  Bargaining power moving the outcome, with no new information and no new
  agreement, is this week's whole argument made real.
