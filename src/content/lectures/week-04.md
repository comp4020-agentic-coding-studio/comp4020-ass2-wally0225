Akerlof's used-car market fails not because sellers of good cars are rare,
but because buyers can't tell good cars from bad ones, so good sellers exit
and the average car for sale gets worse. The same mechanism produces the
"red flag" folk-wisdom: it isn't a personality trait, it's a market
prediction — the pool self-selects toward whoever adverse selection didn't
scare off.

This week reframes red-flag-spotting as adverse-selection detection rather
than character-reading, which is a meaningfully different — and more
teachable — skill.

## Outline

- the market for lemons, worked from the original model
- why "if it seems too good to be true" is adverse selection stated as a
  proverb
- what information asymmetry a red flag is actually evidence of
- why "still available" can mean two opposite things, and the model can't
  tell them apart

## One thing this framework won't explain

The model reads "still on the market" as evidence of low quality — that's
the whole mechanism. But "still available" has (at least) two causes that
look identical from outside the pool: nobody wanted them, or they were
holding out for something specific and turned down offers that would have
taken them off the market. Adverse selection predicts the same observable
pattern either way, and has no term for telling a self-selecting holdout
apart from someone the market has already judged.

That's a third kind of gap this course keeps finding: Week 1's model
couldn't price the value of not being rushed; Week 3's couldn't tell a
credible signal from a relevant one; this one can't tell two different
causes of the same pool apart. Week 12 is where all three come back at
once.
