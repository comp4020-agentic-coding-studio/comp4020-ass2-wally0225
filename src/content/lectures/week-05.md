Signaling puts the burden on the informed party to reveal itself; screening
flips it — the uninformed party designs a mechanism that separates types by
offering a menu only some would rationally choose. An insurer that offers a
low-premium, high-deductible plan alongside a high-premium, low-deductible
one is screening for risk without ever asking anyone's risk category
directly.

Assignment 1 is due this week: having spent three weeks learning to read
signals, you now build one, and defend which of its claims would survive
being screened.

## Outline

- separating equilibria via self-selection, not disclosure
- designing a menu that only reveals type if people choose honestly
- what a screening test would need to look like to actually work on a
  dating profile, and why most "verification" features don't
- what a working menu still costs the honest type, even when it works

## One thing this framework won't explain

A menu that separates types is, by this model's own standard, a success —
the honest type chose one option, the dishonest type chose another, and
the mechanism did its job. What the model has no term for is the honest
type's cost of proving it: the low-risk driver still pays the deductible.
The person who's actually trustworthy still has to sit through whatever
the menu asks of them, and the model treats that cost as fully justified by
the information it produces, without ever asking whether it was fair to
make anyone pay it.

That's the same shape as Weeks 1, 3, and 4's gaps, from a different angle:
the model can certify that a mechanism works and still say nothing about
what it costs the people it works on. Week 12 is where all four of these
come back at once.
