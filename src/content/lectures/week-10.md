The prisoner's dilemma has one Nash equilibrium played once: defect. Played
an unknown number of times against the same partner, the folk theorem says
almost any outcome — including sustained cooperation — can be an
equilibrium too, provided both sides value the future enough and can punish
defection when it happens. Trust, on this account, isn't a feeling; it's
what a repeated game looks like from the inside when the shadow of the
future is long enough to make cooperation self-enforcing.

Tit-for-tat's tournament success is the concrete evidence: a strategy that
cooperates first, then simply mirrors what it was shown, beats far more
elaborate ones — not because it's clever, but because it's legible. It's
instantly readable (your partner knows exactly what triggers what), quick
to forgive (one bad round costs exactly one round of retaliation, not the
whole relationship), and impossible to exploit for more than a single
round at a time. Every one of those properties is about how the strategy
behaves under a partner's mistakes, not how it behaves against a perfectly
rational opponent — which is the more realistic test, since real partners
do have bad weeks.

That last point matters more than it looks. Swap tit-for-tat's forgiveness
for a harsher rule — defect forever after the first defection, no matter
what happens next — and the exact same one-off bad month that tit-for-tat
absorbs in a single round of mutual sulking instead ends the relationship
permanently. The folk theorem doesn't pick between these; both "forgiving
cooperation" and "grim, permanent retaliation" are equilibria once the
shadow of the future is long enough. Which one two real people actually
play is a fact about the strategies they carry into the relationship, not
a fact the model derives for you.

This is also where week 9's negotiated split stops being an abstract split
and starts being something that has to survive Tuesday, and the Tuesday
after that. A split isn't self-enforcing the moment it's signed — nothing
stops either side from quietly taking more the instant they believe it's
the last round. What actually holds a negotiated split together over time
is this week's shadow of the future: the expectation of enough future
rounds that breaking the deal now costs more than whatever it gains.

Priya and Theo, several months into whatever week 6 started, supply this
week's running case: Theo has a standing habit of forgetting to text when
he's running late, and Priya has settled — without ever deciding to,
exactly — into a functioning tit-for-tat, one noticeably cool reply after
a missed heads-up, full warmth restored the instant the next one arrives
on time. Neither of them has read Axelrod. Both of them are, completely
unknowingly, playing the equilibrium the folk theorem predicts should be
stable.

## Outline

- one-shot vs. repeated games, and why only defection survives once
- the folk theorem: repetition doesn't just allow cooperation, it makes
  almost any credible-punishment equilibrium possible
- tit-for-tat, and why legibility and forgiveness both matter more than
  cleverness
- what happens when a strategy's forgiveness is swapped for permanent
  retaliation, using the exact same one-off mistake
- what "the shadow of the future" means for whether a negotiated split
  (week 9) actually holds over time, rather than just on paper

## One thing this framework won't explain

The folk theorem's proof is also its limitation: it shows cooperation
*can* be an equilibrium once the shadow of the future is long enough, but
so can almost anything else two sides can credibly threaten each other
into, including permanent mutual retaliation over a single misread bad
month. The model doesn't say which equilibrium two real people actually
settle into — it only says the set of possible ones is enormous.

That's week 7's gap again, one level up. Week 7 couldn't say who breaks a
standoff on a single night; this week, the same missing ingredient decides
which of many possible relationships two people end up spending years
inside — forgiving or grim, cooperative or locked in retaliation, with the
same starting conditions each time. Whatever actually picks one of these
in practice (a couple's own history, an explicit conversation, plain
personality) is, once again, coming from outside the game.

## Further reading

- Friedman, J. W. (1971).
  ["A Non-cooperative Equilibrium for Supergames."](https://jeromemathis.fr/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Friedman_RES1971.pdf)
  _The Review of Economic Studies_, 38(1), 1–12. An early formal statement
  of what this week calls the folk theorem — repeated play turning a
  single equilibrium into a whole family of them, sustained by the threat
  of future punishment rather than any enforceable contract.
- Axelrod, R., & Hamilton, W. D. (1981).
  ["The Evolution of Cooperation."](https://bibliotecadigital.exactas.uba.ar/Download/Bunge/AR_UBA_FCEN_MB_SecPI_SerFT_193.pdf)
  _Science_, 211(4489), 1390–1396. The tournament result this week's
  tit-for-tat claim is drawn from — a computer tournament where a short,
  forgiving, instantly-readable strategy beat far more elaborate ones
  submitted by game theorists trying to be clever.
