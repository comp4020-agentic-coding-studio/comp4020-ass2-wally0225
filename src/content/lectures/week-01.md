A market is any arrangement where scarce things get allocated among people
who want them, under rules nobody fully controls. Housing is a market.
University admission is a market. This course's claim is that courtship is
one too, and that the tools economists built for the first two — search
costs, signaling, screening, stable matching, mechanism design — say
something true about the third.

The claim is not that romance is "just" economics, or that the vocabulary
of markets improves anyone's love life. It's narrower and, we think, more
interesting: wherever information is unevenly held and outcomes depend on
what other people do, markets fail in specific, nameable ways. The semester
is a tour of those failures, in order of how badly they hurt.

## A market that actually failed

Take a market that isn't romance at all. Before 1950, US hospitals hired
newly graduated doctors into residency positions through an informal
process: interview, then wait for an offer. Competition for the strongest
candidates pushed each hospital to make its offer earlier than the last
one did — by 1945 offers were going out before students had finished the
relevant year of study. Agreeing on a fixed date didn't fix it; it just
moved the deadline everyone raced to beat. By 1949 hospitals were making
"exploding" offers — decide within a day or two, with no way to know what
else might have come along and no time to compare. Nobody in this market
was behaving irrationally. Every hospital jumping the gun was responding
correctly to every other hospital already jumping the gun. The market
unraveled anyway, and stayed unraveled until a redesign — a centralized
clearinghouse taking ranked preferences from both sides at once — replaced
it in the early 1950s (Roth 1984). Week 8 runs that redesign's actual
algorithm by hand.

Courtship has its own version of the same failure, and it doesn't need a
label to be recognizable: the pressure to "lock things down" before you've
had a real chance to compare, because taking the time to compare reads, to
the other person, as a lack of commitment rather than as due diligence.
Nobody involved has to be acting badly for the whole arrangement to end up
earlier and less informed than anyone actually wanted.

## One thing this framework won't explain

Redesigning the residency market fixed the timing — it didn't ask whether
earlier certainty was something residents actually wanted for its own
sake, only whether the market allocating it was efficient. This course's
model is built the same way. It can tell you when a courtship market is
unraveling and roughly what a better mechanism would look like, but it has
no term for whether the anxiety of being rushed, or the relief of finally
being chosen, is worth anything beyond the inefficiency it's a symptom of.
That gap — between what a market model prices and what it doesn't even
have a word for — isn't a footnote. It's Week 12's actual subject, and
this is the first of several places this semester where you'll be asked to
notice the framework going quiet.

## Further reading

Real economics, not romance, but the case study above is drawn straight
from it:

- Roth, A. E. (1984).
  ["The Evolution of the Labor Market for Medical Interns and Residents: A Case Study in Game Theory."](https://web.stanford.edu/~alroth/papers/evolut.pdf)
  _Journal of Political Economy_, 92(6), 991–1016.
- Roth, A. E., & Xing, X. (1994).
  ["Jumping the Gun: Imperfections and Institutions Related to the Timing of Market Transactions."](https://web.stanford.edu/~alroth/papers/RothAndXing1994.pdf)
  _American Economic Review_, 84(4), 992–1044.
- Stanford Magazine, ["The Visible Hand"](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/the-visible-hand) —
  a readable account of Roth's work on unraveling markets, for anyone who'd
  rather start with the story than the journal article.
